Advanced Course: Seed Storage for Business Reserves

On-chain multisig storage for a shared business reserve controlled by two owners

Overview

What this guide teaches:

This course focuses only on the deepest layer of a small business Bitcoin setup: the long-term on-chain reserve.

This course is not about the customer-facing payment systems or daily operating procedures.

For detailed information on those topics, visit the next course: Bitcoin Business Operations.

The target model is a shared business reserve where two owners hold keys to a multisig wallet. The reserve is separate from day-to-day funds and is used for retained earnings, savings, or strategic Bitcoin holdings that should not move casually.

Both owners may also keep separate personal Bitcoin wallets or personal reserves outside the shared business reserve. The shared multisig protects company funds; it does not need to replace each owner's own wallet or private savings plan.

What You'll Build

  • A clean boundary between the shared business reserve and personal owner wallets
  • An on-chain multisig policy for two owners who jointly control reserve funds
  • A seed and descriptor backup plan that can survive device loss and owner absence
  • A documented signing process for withdrawals from the reserve
  • A review schedule for recovery tests, key replacement, and governance changes

Stage 1: Reserve Scope

Stage 1.1: Shared reserve vs personal wallets

The first design decision is what the business reserve is allowed to contain. A shared reserve should hold Bitcoin that belongs to the business, not every coin controlled by either owner.

This distinction matters because the reserve has a different purpose from a personal wallet. The shared reserve is governed by both owners and should require deliberate coordination to spend. Personal wallets can remain under each owner's own control, with their own backup and privacy choices.

Shared business reserve

  • Holds company savings and retained earnings
  • Uses a documented multisig policy
  • Requires owner agreement before spending
  • Has formal recovery and review procedures

Separate owner wallets

  • Hold personal Bitcoin outside the company reserve
  • Can use a different wallet design and privacy model
  • Do not give the other owner control over private savings
  • Should not be mixed with business accounting records

Stage 1.2: What belongs in deep storage

Deep storage is for funds that do not need to move quickly. If the business expects to use some Bitcoin for short-term expenses, refunds, supplier payments, or operational liquidity, those funds should live in a separate operating wallet or business reserve layer outside this course.

The deeper reserve should be boring by design. It should receive planned deposits and make rare, documented withdrawals. That discipline keeps the owners from exposing long-term savings during normal business activity.

Stage 2: Multisig Model

Stage 2.1: Two-owner reserve control

For two owners, the usual starting point is a simple on-chain multisig wallet where no single everyday device can move the reserve by itself. The goal is to prevent unilateral mistakes, theft from one device, and accidental loss from one failed backup.

A common policy is 2-of-3 multisig: Owner A controls one signer, Owner B controls one signer, and a third signer or recovery key is stored separately. Spending normally requires both owners, while recovery remains possible if one signer is lost.

A stricter 2-of-2 multisig can enforce full agreement, but it has less tolerance for key loss. For many small businesses, 2-of-3 is easier to operate responsibly because it gives the owners a recovery path without letting one owner spend alone.

That last sentence is only true if the third key is controlled carefully. If Owner A can access Owner A's signer plus the recovery signer, Owner A can spend. If Owner B can access Owner B's signer plus the recovery signer, Owner B can spend. A 2-of-3 wallet therefore needs a recovery mechanic that prevents either owner from assembling the third key alone.

This is a fundamental trust problem. A business could choose a design where both owners trust each other completely and either owner can recover with the third key, but this course does not recommend relying on that assumption. The goal is to minimize the amount of trust required between owners, especially as the reserve grows.

If the reserve becomes valuable enough, the recovery key can become a theft incentive. Owner A or Owner B may decide that accessing the recovery key is worth the risk, then combine it with their own signer to move funds without the other owner's consent. The setup can be adjusted later when the value rises, but it is better to solve this governance problem before the reserve ever reaches that point.

Stage 2.2: Keys, devices, and policy

Each signer should be generated and backed up independently. Do not create all keys on one laptop and then hand them out. Each owner should understand which signer they control, where its seed backup is stored, and what information is needed to reconstruct the multisig wallet.

The best starting point is for each signer to be created independently with a stateless signing device such as SeedSigner, using real physical randomness from the outside world. Coin flips, dice rolls, or another auditable source of entropy are preferable to blindly trusting a device's internal random number generator. In the basic cold-wallet course, you learned this process in Stage 4.1.1: Creating your own entropy using a coin or dice.

Item Recommended rule
Owner A signer Generated and backed up by Owner A, stored away from Owner B's signer
Owner B signer Generated and backed up by Owner B, stored away from Owner A's signer
Recovery signer Controlled by a mechanism that prevents either owner from accessing it alone
Wallet descriptor Backed up by both owners and by the party holding key C so the multisig wallet can be reconstructed
Spending policy Written down before meaningful funds enter the reserve

Stage 2.3: Recovery key control

The recovery signer exists to solve a real problem: the business still needs a path forward if one owner dies, disappears, becomes incapacitated, loses a signer, or refuses to cooperate with a legitimate recovery process. But the recovery signer also creates a new risk. If one owner can get it alone, that owner can combine it with their own signer and take the reserve.

The course cannot decide the legal or personal conditions for your business. Owner A and Owner B must discuss those conditions themselves and document them with the right professional help where needed. The technical requirement is simpler: the third key should require a recovery process, not unilateral access by one owner.

Starting questions for the owners

  • When is the recovery signer allowed to be used?
  • Who confirms that an owner is dead, incapacitated, absent, or legally removed?
  • Which people or institutions are trusted enough to hold recovery material?
  • How do those parties verify that a release request is legitimate?
  • What records should be created when the recovery signer is used?

There are several ways to build the mechanic. The right choice depends on the amount stored, the relationship between the owners, the available trusted parties, and the business's legal setup.

Recovery mechanic Concept Main tradeoff
Split recovery material Owner A's trusted party holds one part of the recovery key material, and Owner B's trusted party holds the other part Neither owner can recover alone, but the trusted parties need clear release rules
Device and secret separation One party holds the third signer device while a different party holds the PIN, passphrase, or recovery instructions Simple to understand, but the split must be tested carefully
Neutral custodian A bank, notary, lawyer, accountant, professional key agent, or other neutral party controls the recovery signer under agreed conditions Cleaner governance, but it depends on the quality and reliability of the custodian
Two trusted-party release Each owner appoints a trusted party, and both trusted parties must cooperate to unlock or release the recovery signer Useful for owner-death scenarios, but the parties must understand their role

Do not treat this as a place for clever secrecy. The owners should be able to explain the recovery mechanic in plain language: normal spending needs both owners, emergency recovery needs the surviving owner plus the agreed recovery process, and no single owner can quietly obtain two keys.

Some owners may be tempted to avoid this work by handing full reserve control to a large bank, custodian, or other institution. That may be operationally possible, and it may allow a company to keep growing while using Bitcoin on its balance sheet. But it defeats much of the purpose of Bitcoin custody: the business no longer minimizes trust, it simply moves full trust to the institution. If that institution can freeze, lose, rehypothecate, censor, or seize the reserve, the attack vector is glaring.

Stage 3: Backup Distribution

Stage 3.1: Seed and descriptor backups

A multisig backup is not only the seed words. To recover the reserve wallet, the owners also need the wallet policy and descriptor information that explains how the signers combine into the same wallet.

Each seed backup should be durable, private, and stored separately from the other seeds. The descriptor backup can be copied more broadly because it does not spend by itself, but it still reveals wallet structure and should be treated as sensitive business information.

A descriptor or xpub backup is not a private key, but it is still not public information. It can reveal the wallet structure and, when connected to blockchain data, can reveal balances, addresses, and transaction history. Treat descriptor backups as business-confidential records, and use your own Bitcoin full node for reserve monitoring where possible instead of leaking reserve addresses to random public servers. This privacy model is introduced in Basic Course 1: Privacy.

Stage 3.2: Location planning

Location planning should avoid two failures: all keys in one place, or so much separation that the owners cannot recover under stress. The reserve should survive a burglary, fire, device failure, or one owner being unavailable without making normal theft easy.

Backup design checks

  • No single location contains enough material to spend alone
  • No single owner can combine their own signer with the recovery signer alone
  • No single lost device destroys access to the reserve
  • Both owners have the descriptor and recovery instructions
  • Seed backups are labelled carefully without advertising their value
  • The recovery signer has a clear access rule for emergencies

Stage 4: System Model Draft

Before choosing the exact recovery process, the owners should draft the basic system model on one page. This draft does not need to be perfect. It should make the assumptions visible before legal documents, trusted people, institutions, or real funds are added.

System model draft

  • Who controls signer A, signer B, and signer C?
  • Where are each seed backup, signer device, PIN, passphrase, and descriptor stored?
  • Which single person or location could currently assemble two keys?
  • Which events allow signer C to be used?
  • Who verifies those events and who must be notified?
  • How does the reserve move to a new wallet after recovery?
  • Which assumptions depend on personal trust instead of technical or legal separation?
  • Which company records, shareholder agreements, heir documents, accounting rules, or tax records need to match this custody model?

This stage is also where the owners identify legal and accounting boundaries. This course cannot provide legal or tax advice, but the custody model should match company ownership records, signing authority, inheritance plans, bookkeeping practice, and any contracts that define what happens when an owner leaves, dies, or is removed.

Stage 5: Recovery Process Workflow

This stage is a practical worksheet for designing the recovery process around key C. The goal is not to make the third key easy to access. The goal is to make it available only under the exact emergency conditions the owners agreed to in advance.

Stage 5.1: Recovery trigger map

Start by listing every situation where the recovery key may be used. The owners should define the trigger before choosing people, institutions, or storage locations.

Trigger questions

  • Is recovery allowed when one signer device is lost but both owners are available?
  • Is recovery allowed when one owner dies, disappears, or becomes incapacitated?
  • Is recovery allowed when one owner is removed from the company by contract or court order?
  • Who confirms that the trigger event is real?
  • What documents prove the trigger: death certificate, court order, shareholder decision, medical incapacity document, police report, or notarized statement?
  • Is there a waiting period before the recovery key can be released?
  • Who must be notified before the recovery key is released?
  • Is there a direct heir, executor, or legal representative if one owner disappears or dies?

Stage 5.2: Private trusted person path

If a private trusted person is involved, the owners must decide exactly what that person knows and what they physically hold. A trusted person should not receive vague responsibility. They need a narrow role, written instructions, and a clear release protocol.

Decision Questions to answer
Knowledge Do they know this is a Bitcoin recovery process, or only that they hold a sealed business document or device?
Material Do they hold a signer device, a seed backup, a PIN, a passphrase, a descriptor, or only instructions?
Partial control Can their material combine with one owner signer to spend, or is another person or document required?
Release request Who is allowed to ask them for the material, and how does the trusted person verify that identity?
Heir path If an owner dies, does the trusted person release material to the surviving owner, a direct heir, an executor, or a lawyer?
Refusal and conflict What should they do if one owner requests release and the other owner objects?
Record keeping What record do they create when material is released: date, requester, proof checked, witnesses, and destination?

Stage 5.3: Institution path

If a bank, lawyer, notary, accountant, professional key agent, or several institutions are involved, the key question changes from personal trust to enforceable procedure. The contract should define the institution's exact role and should avoid giving one institution quiet, unilateral control over company reserves.

Contract area What it should regulate
Custody scope Exactly what the institution holds: device, seed words, sealed envelope, descriptor, PIN, passphrase, or instructions.
Release conditions Which events allow release and which documents must be presented before release.
Multi-party approval Whether one institution can release alone, or whether two institutions, two trustees, or a trustee plus legal document must agree.
Notification duty Who must be notified before release, how long they have to object, and how notices are delivered.
Dispute handling What happens if owners, heirs, or officers disagree about whether recovery is legitimate.
Continuity What happens if the institution closes, merges, loses records, changes policy, or refuses service.
Audit and testing How the company verifies the material still exists without exposing enough information to spend.
Liability Who is responsible if the institution releases too early, refuses a valid release, or loses material.

Stage 5.4: Recovery protocol draft

After answering the questions above, write the recovery protocol as an ordered checklist. It should be simple enough that a stressed owner, heir, or professional can follow it without improvising.

Minimum recovery protocol

  • Name the normal signers, the recovery signer, and the descriptor backup locations
  • Define every recovery trigger and the proof required for each trigger
  • State who may request recovery and who must be notified
  • State exactly what each private person or institution holds
  • State exactly when and how release happens
  • Define how heirs, executors, or legal representatives are identified
  • Define what record is created when material is released
  • Define how the reserve is moved to a new wallet after recovery
  • Test the protocol with a small amount before relying on it

Stage 6: Wallet Rehearsal

Stage 6.1: Signing and withdrawal rules

Before the reserve receives meaningful funds, define how withdrawals are approved. Write down who can propose a transaction, what business reason is required, how the destination address is verified, and which records are kept after signing.

The signing process should be slow enough to catch mistakes. Owners should verify the receive address on trusted screens, compare transaction details independently, and keep a short record of why the withdrawal happened. This address-verification ceremony is mandatory before any large deposit: both owners compare the receive address against the same multisig descriptor and wallet policy before funds move.

Stage 6.2: Air-gapped wallet test

Test the setup before storing serious funds. Build the wallet, receive a small amount, recover the wallet from backups, sign a test spend, and confirm both owners understand the steps. This stage is only a rehearsal with small funds. Real reserve funding belongs in Stage 7 after the recovery process and test signing have been proven.

Practical task: air-gapped reserve wallet rehearsal

  • Create three independent entropy sets for signer A, signer B, and signer C. Use real physical randomness such as coin flips or dice rolls, and calculate the seed words with SeedSigner without typing seed material into an internet-connected device.
  • Load signer A into SeedSigner and export its public key information as an animated QR or other air-gapped export. Repeat separately for signer B and signer C.
  • Create the 2-of-3 multisig wallet in Sparrow Wallet on the coordinator computer. Sparrow should act as the watch-only coordinator wallet: it may know the wallet policy, public keys, receive addresses, UTXOs, and descriptor, but it must not receive any seed words or private keys. This course uses Sparrow Wallet as the default coordinator, but the owners may choose another multisig-capable wallet or coordinator if they understand its backup format, PSBT workflow, and recovery requirements.
  • Connect Sparrow to the business's own Bitcoin full node where possible. A public server can learn reserve addresses and balances from a watch-only wallet, even though it cannot spend funds. Review Basic Course 1: Privacy if the owners need to understand why the node matters for on-chain reserve privacy.
  • Back up the wallet descriptor before funds are added. Both owners and the key C holder should have the descriptor backup needed to reconstruct the multisig wallet.
  • Recover the Sparrow watch-only wallet from the descriptor on a second device or clean wallet profile. Confirm that the same receive addresses appear.
  • Send a small initial test amount to the reserve wallet. Both owners verify the receive address through Sparrow and against the exported descriptor information before sending.
  • Create a small spend from the reserve in Sparrow. Keep the transaction unsigned on the online coordinator, move the PSBT to SeedSigner by QR, sign with signer A, then sign with signer B. Sparrow receives the signed PSBT and broadcasts only after both owners verify destination address, amount, and fee.
  • Repeat the spend test with one owner signer plus signer C. This confirms the recovery path works technically, but it should happen under the test protocol before meaningful funds are stored.
  • Recover the wallet again while the remaining test funds are still on it. Rebuild the watch-only wallet from descriptor backups, reload the required SeedSigner seeds from backups, and confirm the funds and transaction history are visible.
  • Document every step: which signers were used, where descriptor backups are stored, which recovery combination was tested, transaction IDs, and what the owners would do differently before larger deposits.
Review event Required action
New reserve wallet created Test receive, descriptor restore, and small withdrawal before larger deposits
Signer device lost Assess exposure, use the recovery path, and replace the signer if needed
Owner relationship changes Move funds to a new policy before trust assumptions become unclear
Annual review Confirm storage locations, documentation, signer health, and recovery access

Stage 7: Operating Protocols

This stage turns the wallet design into business procedure. The owners should decide the order of actions before funds move, so staff, owners, and outside parties are not inventing policy during normal operations or emergencies.

Stage 7.1: Initial funding protocol

Before the first meaningful deposit, write the source-of-funds and purchase path. The business should know whether reserve Bitcoin comes from retained earnings, owner contributions, customer receipts, treasury purchases, or transfers from an older company wallet.

Use these actor categories when drawing process maps: Owner A, Owner B, Keyholder C, Employee, and Customer. Not every process uses every actor, but every protocol should make clear who initiates, who verifies, who signs, who records, and who must not have access.

Initial funding action order

  • Confirm Stage 6 is complete: descriptor restore, A+B spend, owner-plus-key-C recovery spend, and recovery with funds visible
  • Choose the funding source: retained earnings, owner contribution, treasury purchase, customer receipts, or transfer from an older company wallet
  • Confirm the purchase path: exchange, broker, OTC desk, direct payment, or transfer from existing business holdings
  • Run the address-verification ceremony again before the first real deposit
  • Document the purchase method, exchange or broker, bank account, invoice, and accounting category
  • Move the first real reserve deposit in a controlled amount before scaling to larger treasury deposits

Stage 7.2: Day-to-day treasury flow

The deep reserve should not receive every small payment directly. Define when operational Bitcoin moves into an intermediary owner-controlled wallet, and when that intermediary wallet moves funds into the deep reserve.

For this seed-storage course, the important node is the Bitcoin full node used for on-chain privacy and verification. A Lightning node is different: it manages payment channels, liquidity, and instant merchant payments. This reserve course does not require Lightning, but the separate Bitcoin Business Operations course does cover business payment flows where Lightning may matter. For Lightning wallets and running a Lightning node, use Advanced Course 5: Lightning (Wallets + Node).

Flow Protocol decision
Customer payments Define whether payments arrive in BTCPay Server, Lightning channels, a hot wallet, or an operating wallet.
Operating consolidation Choose a schedule: daily, weekly, by balance threshold, or after large invoices.
Intermediary wallet Define who controls it, how much may remain there, and when excess moves deeper.
Deep reserve deposit Define a higher threshold or monthly cadence so the reserve only receives deliberate, recorded deposits.
UTXO and fee management Avoid sending many tiny deposits into deep storage. Consolidate deliberately when fees are acceptable and record why consolidation happened.
Accounting record Record transaction ID, source, fiat value at time of transfer, purpose, and approving owner.

Stage 7.3: Employee access and devices

Employees should be able to do their jobs without learning seeds, descriptors, recovery procedures, or reserve balances unless their role truly requires it. Access should be assigned by role, not by convenience.

Role Allowed access Device rule
Cashier or floor staff Create invoices and confirm payment status only Store devices remain on site and are not taken home
Manager Refund workflow, invoice review, and limited operating-wallet visibility No seed entry, no descriptor export, no reserve signer access
Bookkeeper Transaction records, reports, and accounting exports Read-only access where possible
Owners Reserve signing, signer backups, recovery process, and policy updates Signing devices stored separately and used only under documented conditions

Stage 7.4: Incident and review protocols

Write incident protocols before they are needed. A lost phone, compromised checkout device, missing signer, staff departure, or owner conflict should trigger a known action sequence.

Protocols to maintain

  • Lost or stolen staff device: revoke access, rotate credentials, review recent invoices
  • Compromised operating wallet: stop intake, sweep safe funds, replace wallet, document exposure
  • Emergency freeze: if signer A, signer B, signer C, a seed backup, or a descriptor location is suspected compromised, stop new deposits until the owners decide whether a wallet migration is required
  • Signer lost or suspected copied: freeze new deposits, assess whether reserve migration is required
  • Wallet migration: move funds to a new multisig policy after owner departure, signer compromise, recovery key exposure, broken trust assumptions, or a major governance change
  • Employee departure: remove accounts, recover devices, rotate shared credentials
  • Owner absence or dispute: follow the recovery process from Stage 5, not ad hoc negotiation
  • Quarterly review: check access lists, device locations, descriptor backups, and operating balances
  • Annual rehearsal: restore watch-only wallet, verify signers, test a small transaction, and update written procedures

Stage 8: Summary

In this course you separated the deepest business reserve from daily merchant activity and from each owner's personal Bitcoin wallet. The shared reserve is a company asset with its own governance, backup, and recovery process.

You now have the outline of an on-chain multisig reserve where two owners hold keys, a recovery path exists for device loss, and withdrawals happen through a deliberate owner approval process.

Customer-facing payments and operating procedures belong in the separate business operations course.
